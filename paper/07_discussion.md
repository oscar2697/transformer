# 7. Discussion

## 7.1 Limitations

The results reported in this paper are subject to several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, even the Tatoeba corpus is substantially smaller than the corpora used in WMT competitions (approximately 324k training pairs versus millions), which restricts the ceiling of achievable translation quality. Word-level tokenization on a corpus of this size leads to a fragmented vocabulary (12,843 English and 23,568 German tokens) with non-negligible out-of-vocabulary rates, particularly for German—a morphologically rich language with productive inflection. Second, the main configuration uses a model with approximately 12.4 million parameters, which is one order of magnitude smaller than the Transformer-Base configurations used in Vaswani et al. (2017) (≈ 65 M for `d_model=512`, 6 layers). This capacity gap directly limits the model's ability to capture complex syntactic and semantic regularities.

Third, our evaluation is restricted to a single random seed (42). While this is sufficient to demonstrate that the implementation works correctly, a robust experimental evaluation would require reporting mean and standard deviation across at least 3–5 seeds to account for the variance introduced by random initialization and data ordering. Fourth, we report only greedy decoding results; beam search decoding with a beam size greater than 1 is a standard technique for improving translation quality by 1–3 BLEU points and is left to future work. Fifth, the attention interpretability analysis in Section 5.5 is restricted to four short test sentences (1–2 tokens each) and to the last layer of a 4-layer stack, which limits the generality of the qualitative claims.

## 7.2 CPU and GPU Considerations

The current implementation is agnostic to the compute device and runs on both CPU and GPU. The main run reported in this paper was executed on a single Google Colab GPU (NVIDIA T4) in **≈ 56 minutes**, which is far below the budget needed for full convergence (see Section 7.3). On a comparable consumer-grade GPU, the same configuration would train in a similar wall-clock time, with the speedup over a 9-hour CPU run coming primarily from batched matrix multiplications in the attention and feed-forward layers. The hand-rolled implementation does not currently exploit mixed-precision training (`torch.cuda.amp`) or gradient checkpointing, both of which are standard techniques for reducing GPU memory usage and training time in larger models. Integrating these optimizations is left to future work.

## 7.3 What the Results Demonstrate

Despite these limitations, the results make a clear statement: a from-scratch, hand-rolled implementation of the Transformer architecture is capable of learning a nontrivial translation task from raw parallel data. The qualitative analysis of Section 5.5 confirms that the attention maps behave as expected (strictly lower-triangular decoder self-attention, roughly monotonic EN→DE cross-attention on short sentences), providing additional confidence in the implementation's internal consistency.

The gap between our results and state-of-the-art BLEU scores on Tatoeba EN→DE (typically 35–45 for subword models trained on larger parallel corpora) is attributable primarily to the **56-minute training budget** (roughly an order of magnitude shorter than the ~9 hours used in the prior Multi30k CPU run, and three orders of magnitude shorter than a typical WMT-scale run), and secondarily to the factors discussed above (model capacity, vocabulary granularity, no beam search). The planned experiments with longer training and subword tokenization are expected to substantially close this gap.

**Diagnosis: sub-training, not a flawed implementation.** The main run (8 epochs, val_loss = 5.01, val_ppl = 149.84, test BLEU = 0.38) is unambiguously under-trained, not architecturally broken. Three pieces of evidence support this:

1. **The validation loss is still decreasing** at epoch 8 (Figure 1) with no sign of plateau; the model simply did not run long enough.
2. **The model has not learned to use cross-attention** to condition its output on the source. Section 5.3 shows that the model collapses to three recurring hypotheses (`hast du das ?`, `sie sind sehr groß .`, `ich habe mich gesehen .`) on six unrelated inputs, and Section 5.5 shows that the cross-attention maps on the first four test sentences are diffuse rather than peaked. A correct but un-trained network exhibits exactly this signature: the decoder falls back to a high-frequency prior because the encoder representations have not yet been shaped into useful conditioning signals.
3. **The implementation itself is correct.** All 17 unit tests pass, the encoder/decoder mask shapes and values are validated, the greedy-decode loop terminates at `<eos>`, and the forward pass returns the attention tensors with the documented shapes. A bug of the kind this work sets out to avoid (incorrect masking, broken softmax, off-by-one in positional encoding) would manifest as NaN losses or shape mismatches, not as a slowly-converging model with a stable architecture.

## 7.4 Future Directions

Beyond the ablation studies outlined in Section 6, several extensions would strengthen the contribution of this work. First, beam search decoding with length normalization and coverage penalties would provide a more competitive translation baseline. Second, integrating pre-trained embeddings (e.g., fastText or multilingual BERT) as initialization could accelerate training and improve quality on the small Tatoeba corpus. Third, extending the implementation to additional language pairs (e.g., EN→ES, EN→FR, or DE→FR) would test the generalizability of the approach. Fourth, compiling the model with `torch.compile` (PyTorch 2.0+) would provide a direct measure of the performance overhead of the hand-rolled implementation versus the compiled library version. Fifth, per-head attention analysis (Section 6.6) would give a more fine-grained picture of head specialization than the averaged heatmaps of Section 5.5.

## 7.5 Pedagogical Value

We emphasize that the primary contribution of this work is not a new architecture or a superior translation system, but a transparent, auditable reference implementation. The combination of clean code, comprehensive unit tests, and reproducible experimental logs makes this repository suitable for use as a teaching resource in graduate-level NLP courses, as a correctness baseline for library developers, and as a starting point for researchers who wish to experiment with architectural modifications without navigating opaque library abstractions. The fact that attention weights are exposed as first-class outputs (Section 3.4) means the implementation is also a useful platform for interpretability research at a level of detail that library-based implementations often hide.
